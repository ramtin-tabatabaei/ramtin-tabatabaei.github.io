<section class="paper-showcase__panel paper-showcase__panel--overview">
  <h2>Overview</h2>
  <p>This study explores the dynamics of trust in human-robot collaboration in the context of <strong>repeated</strong> robot failures. It focuses on how the consistency of failure types and the robot's awareness of its own errors affect user perceptions of trust and intelligence.</p>
</section>

<section class="paper-showcase__panel paper-showcase__panel--method">
  <h2>Method and System</h2>
  <p>Using a collaborative Tangram puzzle task, I ran a mixed-design experiment with 54 participants. The robot intentionally failed under different conditions, manipulating both the <strong>failure sequence type</strong> (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) and the robot's <strong>level of failure awareness</strong> (none, partial, full) across the failure types Freezing, Planning, and Grasping.</p>
</section>

<section class="paper-showcase__panel paper-showcase__panel--results">
  <h2>What I Found</h2>
  <p>User trust and perceived intelligence are affected by both the current and the previous failure, with homogeneous sequences generally causing a smaller trust decline than heterogeneous ones. Robots that demonstrated awareness — full or partial — were rated significantly higher in trust and perceived intelligence than those showing no awareness, particularly for grasping and planning failures.</p>
</section>

<section class="paper-showcase__panel paper-showcase__panel--discussion">
  <h2>Why It Matters</h2>
  <p>These insights offer design implications for building more resilient and trustworthy human-robot interactions amid inevitable errors. Knowing <em>when</em> failure awareness helps — and when it backfires — lets us design adaptive trust-repair strategies rather than one-size-fits-all apologies.</p>
</section>
