What should be true?
A robot can complete a movement and still fail the task. CoMET monitors what should be true, before, during, and after each action.

From one demonstration to online monitoring
The flow starts with one successful demonstration. It becomes a structured description, then a condition-annotated behaviour tree, which coordinates online monitoring.

Basketball into a hoop
Here, the robot moves towards the basketball, grasps it, transports it, and releases it into the hoop. These are this task’s stages.

Describe the successful task
Side, front, and wrist views are combined with gripper state, held-object identity, and pose information. The model produces a goal, named objects, and ordered waypoint descriptions.

A shared vocabulary. Task-specific checks.
A reusable catalogue defines conditions such as held, released, inside, and aligned with. The six available primitives are move, grasp, transport, place, release, and push.

Different tasks use different primitive sequences
Tasks do not all use the same primitives, or the same number of stages. Basketball uses move, grasp, transport, and release. A pushing task can use move and push, while shelf placement can also include place. Primitives may repeat; objects, conditions, and goals change with the task.

Generate, ground, and review
The generator assigns one primitive per waypoint and grounds its conditions in task-specific objects. A reviewer checks completeness and consistency; fixed rules attach the applicable detectors.

Inside the basketball behaviour tree
Each stage follows the same monitoring structure: check preconditions, execute with applicable monitors, then check postconditions. During transport, the basketball must already be held and remain held.

M2 / Check the expected task state
At checkpoints, a vision-language model evaluates the active conditions using three camera views. One call combines the previous stage’s postconditions with the next stage’s preconditions.

M1 / Monitor physical execution
Robot-state detectors watch for collisions during motion, and grasp loss during transport and place. Position and orientation are checked at waypoint completion. A push stage does not require maintaining a grasp.

The basketball was held. Then it was lost.
In this disturbed basketball recording, the grasp succeeds, but the ball is lost during transport. The task fails before the intended release.

Candidate event → selective visual confirmation
A grasp-loss or collision candidate triggers a targeted visual check. The model receives before-and-after views and recent detector signals to confirm or reject the event. Pose deviations are checked directly.

Some failures are about the task, not the motion
If the robot never grasps the ball, there may be no grasp-loss signal. The visual condition check can still flag that the required object is not held.

A stage, a condition, and supporting evidence
CoMET reports the stage, violated condition, and supporting explanation. Detecting a violation is distinct from naming its cause; benchmark failure labels use mapping rules.

Reported evaluation across 78 tasks
Across seventy-eight tasks, the supplied study reports eighty-three point three percent binary F1 for CoMET. Direct prompting reaches fifty-four point four; adding the same detectors and confirmation reaches eighty-two point eight. CoMET also improves macro F1.

Detection and diagnosis are different goals
Failure detection is stronger than exact diagnosis. Collision types and sequence violations remain challenging, and detector-triggered visual confirmation adds computational cost.

One framework. A task-specific monitoring plan.
The idea is reusable, but the monitoring plan is task-specific. Demonstrate success. Define what must hold. Monitor execution. Explain deviations.
